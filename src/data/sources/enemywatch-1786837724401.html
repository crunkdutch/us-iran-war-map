— Both India and Pakistan are celebrating their Independence Days, with Pakistan observing its Independence Day on August 14 and India on August 15. Yet the celebrations are often accompanied by songs, chants, political rhetoric, and social-media fights directed against one another. Nearly eight decades after Partition, it is worth asking how deeply both nations have forgotten who the original colonial power was, who engineered the transfer of power, and what immense suffering followed the division of the subcontinent. The Partition of British India brought mass displacement and horrific communal violence, with millions uprooted and hundreds of thousands, possibly up to a million, killed.

Instead of allowing the memory of that history to produce serious reflection, India and Pakistan have often turned their rivalry against each other, while the broader history of British colonial rule, its policies, and the rushed process of Partition receives far less attention in everyday political discourse. British records themselves document the escalating communal tensions, the hurried transfer of power, and the enormous displacement and violence that accompanied Partition.

This does not mean that every tragedy after 1947 can simply be attributed to Britain, nor that the responsibilities of Indian and Pakistani political actors should be erased. History is more complicated than that. But the colonial legacy and the circumstances surrounding Partition cannot simply be removed from the story. The hurried drawing of the Radcliffe boundary, the division of Punjab and Bengal, and the resulting mass migration created wounds whose consequences continue to shape India-Pakistan relations today.

So while people celebrate their respective national days, perhaps the more important question is: why are the people of two nations that emerged from the same colonial system still encouraged to see each other primarily through hatred, rivalry, and inherited hostility? Almost eight decades later, the people of the subcontinent deserve to remember the full history, including colonialism, Partition, communal violence, political failures, and the millions of ordinary people who paid the price. The lesson should not be to erase national identities, but to stop allowing historical wounds to be transformed into permanent hatred between neighbouring peoples.
