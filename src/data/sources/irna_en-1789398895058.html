🔊 Iran seeks Qatar’s help in locating three missing pilots

📌 Tehran, IRNA – The head of the Iranian Red Crescent Society (IRCS) has urged Qatar Red Crescent to follow up on the status of three missing pilots through its related channels.


Pirhossein Kolivand emphasized the development of bilateral cooperation between the national societies of Iran and Qatar, calling for pursuing the status of the three Iranian pilots in Qatar through that country’s Red Crescent.
​
He also stressed expanding cooperation in rescue and relief, training, and the exchange of know-how in these fields.
​
This cooperation can be further developed by renewing and updating the previous memorandum of understanding between the Red Crescent of Iran and Qatar, he stated.
​
Kolivand suggested that a draft of a new memorandum of understanding be examined and finalized by the national societies of both countries, with the aim of having it signed in person by the two parties.
​
The Qatari Red Crescent should use its own channels to gather information on the three pilots’ situation, so that, in keeping with the impartial humanitarian mandate of the Red Crescent and Red Cross, the necessary support can be extended to their anxious families, he noted.
​
Earlier, Commander of Iran’s Missing-in-Action Search Committee Brigadier General Mohammad Bagherzadeh had called on the International Committee of the Red Cross to send an air ambulance to Qatar to facilitate the urgent return of the Iranian pilots held there, whom he said are severely injured and in bad health.
​
He said the location where the Iranian prisoners are being held on water is unsuitable, urging the Qatari government to transfer them to land and an adequately equipped hospital as soon as possible to protect their health.

🔻 Wounds caused by US ‘heinous’ crime in Lamerd will never heal: Baqaei

📌 Tehran, IRNA — Foreign Ministry spokesperson Esmaeil Baqaei has emphasized the need for justice for the victims of a February US missile attack on Iran’s southern city of Lamerd, saying that the wounds inflicted by “the heinous crime” will never heal completely.


Baqaei made the comments in a post he shared on X on Sunday night about his daylong trip to the city in Hormuzgan Province a day earlier.
​
During his visit to Lamerd, the spokesperson met with local officials and residents and inspected sites struck in the US attack on February 28, the first day of the recent US-Israeli war of aggression against Iran. He also held a press conference attended by reporters and journalists from Iranian and foreign media outlets, shedding light on the US attack, which he described as a war crime.
​
“During our visit to Lamerd, we saw numerous signs of the US war crime committed on February 28, 2026, not only on the city’s streets, walls and buildings, but also in the bodies and souls of families still bearing the pain of losing their loved ones,” said Baqaei in his post.
​
“Twenty residents of Lamerd and one Afghan immigrant were killed in the explosion of four Lockheed Martin cluster missiles, each packed with 180,000 fragments, which were tested over the city of Lamerd for the first time,” he further said, praising the victims of the US attack, who “sacrificed their lives for our nation’s independence and dignity, are now part of Iran’s enduring national heritage and a source of our national strength and unity.”
​
“Another 180 residents of Lamerd were injured by missile fragments. Even six months after the crime, several Lamerd boys and girls, together with their families, continue to endure disabilities and severe pain and suffering caused by their wounds. Some tungsten pellets from Precision Strike Missiles (PrSMs) have remained lodged in the bodies of some of our children,” the spokesperson said.
​
He stressed that the tragedy in Lamerd should never be forgotten and that the United States must be held accountable for the crime it committed to compensate for its defeat against the Iranian nation.
​
“What happened to the people of Lamerd must never be forgotten. Every martyr must have their story told, every witness must be heard, and every injury and loss must be documented. Documentation is not merely about recording the past; it is about preserving the truth in evidence before it is buried beneath the dust of distortion and oblivion.”
​
“We will collect this documentation to seek justice and hold to account before international forums those responsible for, those who ordered and facilitated the crime. It will serve as evidence of the brutality of aggressors who claim to champion human rights, yet stopped at nothing in committing crimes against humanity to make up for their defeat,” he noted.
​
The diplomat concluded his post by saying, “The wounds inflicted by such a heinous crime will never fully heal. They must be entrusted to memory, evidence and justice. And this is apart from a great disgrace that will forever hang like a curse over their tragic fate.”

🔊 Iran para shooting team wins bronze at world championships

📌 Tehran, IRNA — Iran’s national para shooting team won a bronze medal in the mixed 50m pistol team event at the 2026 World Shooting Para Sport Championships in Changwon, South Korea, bringing the country’s medal tally to four.


Iran’s team of Nasrin Shahi, Seyyed Mohammadreza Mirshafiei and Sareh Javanmardi scored 1,596 points to finish third behind South Korea and China.
​
In the individual event, Shahi advanced to the final after finishing second in the qualifying round with 543 points. She scored 139.6 points in the final to place sixth. Mirshafiei, Javanmardi and Mostafa Khadem, who scored 530, 523, and 510 points, respectively, failed to advance to the final.
​
The Changwon championships marked the end of Iran’s campaign, with the country securing four medals: a team gold, an individual gold, an individual silver, and a team bronze.
​
Faezah Ahmadi also secured a quota place for the 2028 Paralympic Games in Los Angeles.
​
Iran competed under the name of martyr Mohammad Fattahi, with Fathollah Delfani Khan as head coach, assisted by coaches Zeinab Amini-Tabar and Zahra Ahmadi, and Dr. Nasiri as team manager.

💬 Electronic visa process begins for Iranian Hajj 2027 pilgrims

📌 Tehran, IRNA – The electronic visa process for Iranian pilgrims planning to perform Hajj 2027 has begun, with the first Raqam Talab code issued following coordination between Iran and Saudi Arabia, an Iranian official said.


Akbar Rezaei, deputy head of Hajj and Umrah affairs at Iran’s Hajj and Pilgrimage Organization, said the first Raqam Talab had been issued following coordination with Saudi Arabia’s Ministry of Hajj and Umrah.
​
The Raqam Talab is a code issued after applicants’ passport information is uploaded to Saudi Arabia’s Ministry of Hajj and Umrah system. It serves as a preliminary step toward issuing the pilgrim’s electronic visa.
​
Rezaei described the issuance of the first code as a milestone and expressed hope that bilateral cooperation would allow electronic visas for Iranian pilgrims and Hajj staff to be issued smoothly and on schedule.
​
The previous Hajj season faced visa-related difficulties following the outbreak of the recently imposed war on Iran.
​
Iran had planned to send 86,700 pilgrims, while 82,000 Raqam Talab codes had been issued before the conflict.
​
Ultimately, the number of Iranian pilgrims fell to 30,672, while 1,200 people were unable to join the pilgrimage because their codes or visas were not issued.

💬 Iran’s Hormozgan: Where the sea, culture and memory live side by side

📌 Bandar Abbas, IRNA – Hormozgan, a land of diverse colors, dialects, traditions and lives intertwined with the sea, is a place where culture is not merely a relic of the past, but something that continues to breathe in local markets, the lives of fishermen, southern music and the stories passed down through generations.


A land can be known through its map, borders, cities, mountains and seas. But to truly understand a place, one must meet its people, listen to their languages and dialects, witness their traditions and trace history through their everyday lives.
​
Hormozgan is more than a collection of islands, coastlines and southern cities. It is a land of human stories, shaped by generations who have lived alongside the sea, adapted to nature and passed their experiences down through language, music, clothing, customs and ways of life.
​
The sea has played a defining role in this identity. The Persian Gulf and the Sea of Oman have influenced the livelihoods and culture of the people of Hormozgan for centuries. Fishing, seafaring and trade have become integral parts of their way of life, while generations of fishermen have acquired knowledge of winds, waves, seasons and the best times to venture into the sea through experience.
​
Much of this knowledge has been passed from parent to child and from one generation to the next. It represents an intangible cultural heritage that could gradually disappear if traditional occupations and lifestyles fade.
​
Geography has also shaped the province’s architecture, clothing, food and daily life. Windcatchers, wooden windows and traditional houses reflect the experience of people who learned over centuries how to adapt to the hot and humid climate of southern Iran.
​
Anthropology seeks to understand such connections between people, their environment, history and society. It examines customs, beliefs, language, dialects, lifestyles and social relations, offering a deeper understanding of how cultures develop and become part of a community’s identity.
​
The Persian Gulf Anthropology and Archaeology Museum in Bandar Abbas presents part of this heritage. Located in the historic Bekehay-e Baran complex (Ponds of Rain Complex), the museum uses local architectural elements and displays aspects of traditional markets, fishing and the history of Hormozgan, while local voices and dialects bring its exhibits closer to the living culture of the province.
​
Local markets themselves remain another expression of that culture. Beyond trade, they are places where people meet, exchange news and maintain social ties. For an anthropologist, such markets can be seen as living books, with people and their relationships forming their pages.
​
Hormozgan is, ultimately, a vast book of human stories — part of it preserved in museums and historical records, and much of it still alive in the voices of fishermen, local markets, traditional ceremonies and the dialects spoken in homes.
​
Understanding a land is impossible without understanding its people. Hormozgan’s story is one of humanity reflected in culture and the sea — a living story whose next chapter is being written every day.
