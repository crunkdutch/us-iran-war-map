🇾🇪 📕 | The story behind Ansar Allah’s missile arsenal is much deeper than “Iran gave them missiles”:

When people look at Ansar Allah’s missile arsenal in Yemen today, it is easy to reduce the entire story to a single sentence: “Iran supplied the Houthis with missiles.” That explanation ignores an entire layer of Yemen’s military history and, more importantly, it ignores the one element that cannot simply be imported: human expertise.

The foundation of Ansar Allah’s missile capability was not created after 2015. For decades, the Yemeni military operated Soviet, North Korean, and Chinese missile systems. These weapons did not arrive alone. They came with training programs, technical education, maintenance procedures, launch protocols, and generations of Yemeni officers, engineers, and technicians who learned how to manage missile systems from storage and servicing to deployment and refurbishment.

A significant part of that expertise was already close to Ansar Allah even before it took control of state institutions. Military units, officers, engineers, and technicians within the Yemeni Armed Forces formed alliances with Ansar Allah during the political and military shifts that preceded 2014. This meant that experienced personnel with years of missile knowledge became part of Ansar Allah’s structure, rather than a completely new cadre appearing after the collapse of Abd Rabbuh Mansur Hadi’s government in Sana’a.

That point became decisive after 2014. When large parts of the Saudi-backed government collapsed and Ansar Allah seized military bases and stockpiles belonging to the Yemeni Army and the Republican Guard, they inherited much more than warehouses filled with weapons. They gained missile platforms, spare parts, maintenance equipment, technical documentation, engineers, and technicians who already understood those systems from the inside.

This is where the real transformation began. Instead of treating the inherited arsenal as a finite stockpile that would eventually run out, Ansar Allah turned it into the foundation of a missile program that could be repaired, rebuilt, modified, and expanded. Some missiles were restored to service, others were overhauled and modernized, while older systems became platforms for experimentation and redevelopment. This also explains why some Soviet-era systems have reportedly been adapted for missions very different from those for which they were originally designed, including the conversion of certain air defense systems into ground attack weapons after extensive modifications.

None of this means Ansar Allah developed everything on its own. As the war continued and older stockpiles were depleted, support from Iran and Hezbollah became increasingly important, particularly in technical development. The relationship is better understood as cooperation between an existing Yemeni military foundation and external expertise that contributed new technologies, components, engineering methods, and approaches to modernization.

In other words, the Yemenis already possessed the basic knowledge of missile systems, while support from the Axis of Resistance added new layers of expertise in upgrading, modifying, improving performance, and reusing equipment that could be salvaged from older arsenals.

That is what makes Ansar Allah’s missile program different from the simplistic claim that it merely received missiles from abroad. Its development is the result of accumulation: a Yemeni missile legacy built over decades, military stockpiles transferred into Ansar Allah’s hands after 2014, experienced personnel who remained within the new military structure, years of war that forced constant maintenance, refurbishment, and innovation, and external technical support that helped transform an inherited arsenal into a more diverse and sophisticated missile force.
