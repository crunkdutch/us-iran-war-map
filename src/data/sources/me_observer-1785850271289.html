⚡️ Is martyrdom truly synonymous with infallibility in work and activity?
I am Mehdi Shahsavar; please refrain from attributing this text to anyone else.
🔹If I were to speak about the status of a martyr, the dignity of martyrdom, and its relation to our judgments, I must honestly admit that entering such a domain is not easy for me. This discussion is not merely a political, military, or managerial issue; it is intertwined with religious belief, collective emotion, historical memory, and the deep respect of society for those who sacrificed their lives for ideals, homeland, and creed. Therefore, judging them must be accompanied by caution, deference, and humility. I also consider myself in need of further study and education in religious matters and do not intend to issue a definitive ruling on the martyr's otherworldly status or spiritual rank. However, there is a question that, due to its emotional weight, cannot be avoided indefinitely: Does the martyrdom of a commander immunize all their previous decisions, evaluations, plans, and actions from any criticism and review?
🔹More clearly, if someone ultimately achieves martyrdom, should we conclude that all their past choices were correct, all their analyses accurate, all the structures they created flawless, and all the paths shaped by their decisions were beyond question and criticism? Does martyrdom, which undoubtedly can be a sign of the pinnacle of a human's sincerity, sacrifice, and devotion, automatically create a kind of "managerial and operational infallibility" for them? From the moment of martyrdom, are potential errors in policymaking, command, organization, training, threat assessment, resource allocation, or scenario design also excluded from scrutiny? In my opinion, conflating these two realms not only does not help preserve the martyrs' honor but can also lead to the paralysis of organizational rationality and the cessation of historical learning.
🔹There is a fundamental difference between a human's spiritual status and the correctness of all their professional decisions. A person may be at a very high level of sincerity, courage, loyalty, and sacrifice, but as a commander, manager, planner, or decision-maker, they may still have made errors, underestimated situations, been overly optimistic, had a weak understanding of the enemy, suffered from intelligence deficiencies, or made mistakes in prioritization. A faithful, sincere, and self-sacrificing person is still human; limited by the data available to them, the structure in which they operated, the mental atmosphere of their time, and assumptions whose incorrectness might later become apparent. Martyrdom can add to the spiritual value of their sacrifice, but it does not remove the organizational past from its human and fallible nature.
