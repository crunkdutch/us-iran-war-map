Imam Mujtaba Khamenei is an established jurist, recognized within the Assembly of Experts, among other scholars and relevant scholarly circles. His recognition as a jurist predates his announcement as the Leader. Even before assuming the leadership, he was…
Meanwhile, one must also be aware that the martyred Leader, Imam Sayyid Ali Khamenei, had expanded the role and nature of marja' al-taqlid in a broader and somewhat different way, making it more inclusive and accessible than a model limited to followers under a single authority and a single series of risalah amaliyah. The martyred Imam did not actively promote his own marja'iyyah or invite followers in major countries where his following could have expanded. This appears to have been intentional, allowing a greater degree of ikhlas (sincerity and spiritual purity) and inkisari (humility and self-effacement), while avoiding an excessive personality-centered model of religious emulation.

As a marja' al-taqlid himself, he had millions of muqallids, reportedly second only to Ayatollah Sistani in terms of the scale of his following. Yet he maintained an open approach in which many of his risalahs and books incorporated or acknowledged the opinions of other maraji'. He also allowed his followers to refer to other maraji' on specific issues where appropriate. This approach created a more flexible model of taqlid, in which adherence to one marja' did not necessarily mean complete exclusion of the scholarly opinions of others.

Ayatollah Sistani has similarly maintained a position that allows his followers, under certain circumstances, to refer to the a'lam among other qualified maraji' for specific questions or issues, particularly where particular rulings or sensitive matters require further consideration. This demonstrates that the practical tradition of taqlid has not always been as rigidly centralized as it is sometimes portrayed.

With the passing of the martyred Imam, the question now becomes more significant: how will this broader concept of taqlid and reference to multiple maraji' develop under Imam Mujtaba Khamenei? It will be particularly interesting to observe whether his approach continues the practice of allowing followers to consult other recognized maraji' for specific rulings, or whether he develops a distinct doctrinal framework for contemporary marja'iyyah.

There are already indications that his jurisprudential approach may be both clear and comprehensive, with rulings that sometimes resemble those of his father while providing broader explanations and greater elaboration. His discussions during dars al-kharij and meetings with scholars and experts may offer further insight into how he understands the relationship between his own jurisprudential authority, the opinions of other maraji', and the practical needs of Muslims seeking guidance.

Ultimately, the development of marja'iyyah under Imam Mujtaba Khamenei may become one of the significant questions for the contemporary Shi'a scholarly establishment. The issue is not simply who will be followed, but what model of religious emulation will emerge: whether it will remain centered around a single personality, develop as a more plural and interconnected system of qualified authorities, or establish a new balance between individual marja'iyyah and broader collective scholarly consultation.
