🏴‍☠️ | America’s eyes over Iran: Is anything really hidden from satellites?

When people talk about American spy satellites, they often imagine a single satellite continuously watching one location. The reality is much more complex. America operates a multi-layered intelligence network that combines classified National Reconnaissance Office (NRO) satellites, a growing constellation of smaller military satellites, and commercial satellite imagery that is integrated into the American intelligence system. In recent years, the NRO has launched more than 200 satellites, and its new architecture reportedly carried out over 400,000 intelligence collections during 2025, with the goal of reducing revisit times and maintaining continuous surveillance over high-priority targets.

Even commercially available American satellites such as WorldView can capture imagery with a ground resolution of about 30 centimeters, making vehicles, aircraft, military equipment, and even relatively small changes on the ground clearly visible. The actual capabilities of America’s most advanced military reconnaissance satellites remain classified, and any specific claims about their maximum resolution are not publicly verified.

However, this does not mean every house or every location inside Iran is being watched 24 hours a day. Most reconnaissance satellites operate in low Earth orbit and pass over the same location periodically. Optical satellites are also limited by darkness, cloud cover, viewing angles, and mission priorities. At many times, no new optical image of a particular location may be collected at all.

What has changed is the concept of persistent surveillance. Instead of relying on a single satellite, America can combine observations from multiple optical satellites, SAR radar satellites that can image through clouds and at night, infrared sensors that detect heat signatures, and RF intelligence satellites that monitor electronic emissions. For high-priority military or intelligence targets, these systems can be coordinated so the gap between observations becomes much shorter, making concealment significantly more difficult.

The NRO itself has acknowledged that its surveillance capacity is not unlimited, but says its new satellite architecture is designed to deliver intelligence to military users within minutes instead of hours for certain missions. In practice, an ordinary location in Iran is not under uninterrupted surveillance, while strategically important targets can be monitored with overlapping sensors that greatly increase the frequency of observation and reduce opportunities to remain hidden.
