Iran and Iraq

Qalibaf’s visit to Baghdad today comes amid a complex set of issues. According to high-level sources cited by Al-Akhbar, discussions are expected to cover regional security, economic relations, the passage of Iraqi oil through the Strait of Hormuz, and the issue of factional weapons, including Iran’s position on Baghdad’s approach to the matter. The visit comes as the Iraqi government seeks a formula on weapons that could prevent an internal political and security crisis while easing American pressure. In this context, Badr Organization leader Hadi al-Amiri called for moderation and an end to inflammatory rhetoric, stressing that restricting weapons is an exclusively Iraqi matter and that any decision must be made by Iraqis, while warning against giving external actors grounds to interfere in Iraq’s security, political, and economic affairs.

Yemen and Saudi Arabia

News sources reported that Sanaa forces have placed Aramco, including its facilities, installations, oil-storage tanks, crude-oil pipelines, and export ports, on their target list. They warned that Saudi Arabia’s refusal to meet Yemeni demands could put the country’s oil infrastructure at risk. Sanaa forces reportedly continue to restrict Saudi maritime access through the Red Sea and Bab al-Mandab Strait and intend to prevent Saudi vessels from passing until Riyadh lifts its naval and aerial blockade of Yemen and fulfills its obligations. Yemeni navigation sources in Hodeidah reported that daily traffic through Bab al-Mandab has increased to around 30 vessels, but said that naval and coastal-defense forces have not permitted Saudi oil or other Saudi-flagged vessels to pass through the strait in recent weeks. They also said that Saudi ships and oil tankers in the Red Sea continue to be monitored and tracked.

Iran and Gulf states

Chief of Staff of the Iranian Armed Forces, Major General Abdollahi, warned Gulf states that declare they will not allow the United States to use their territory against Iran that Tehran is monitoring developments closely. He said it was unlikely that such a large number of military aircraft, particularly aerial-refueling aircraft, could be deployed at regional bases without the knowledge of their host countries. He warned that any assistance or facilitation provided to U.S. military operations would be considered participation alongside U.S. forces.

Strait of Hormuz and global shipping

Shipping through the Strait of Hormuz slowed further on Wednesday as most shipowners continued avoiding the waterway amid uncertainty over when it would reopen. Six commodity vessels crossed the strait on Tuesday, down from nine the previous day and below the 10-day daily average of 11. The lack of clear signals regarding the waterway’s reopening has kept shipping companies cautious, with traffic remaining far below normal levels. The disruption is also putting pressure on global energy markets, with oil prices rising amid concerns over exports through the strategic waterway. Iraq has begun examining alternative routes for crude exports, while some Chinese shipping companies have also rerouted vessels to avoid both the Strait of Hormuz and Bab al-Mandeb.

🇮🇶 | Abbas Hussein al-Yaqoubi is free now. However, the incident raises questions that go far beyond the arrest itself.

The Counter-Terrorism Service arrested Abbas Hussein al-Yaqoubi, the intelligence chief of the Baghdad Operations Command and one of the senior cadres of Harakat al-Nujaba, from his home in the city of Nasiriyah. The significance of the incident lies not only in the arrest itself, but also in the manner in which it was carried out and the level of the targeted figure. Government forces have previously arrested members of the Resistance factions, but most of those cases took place in the field or inside faction headquarters. Raiding the home of a senior leadership figure is a much bolder step and, in the context of the relationship between the state and the factions, carries a message that goes beyond the individual himself.

The government narrative communicated to the other parties is that al-Yaqoubi is accused of participating in drone attacks during the recent war. But the importance of the man does not lie solely in his possible participation in offensive operations. More important is what he may know about the logistical infrastructure behind them, including drone storage locations, manufacturing sites, transportation networks, and routes used to bring these systems into Iraq. The arrest therefore becomes an attempt to obtain something more valuable than an individual confession: an attempt to dismantle an entire network. This may also be connected to the failed attempt to storm one of al-Nujaba’s headquarters in Jurf al-Naddaf, during which the search focused on missiles and drones. When direct access to the materials fails, reaching individuals believed to hold the keys to this network becomes an alternative option.

But the femboy Zaidi government was not merely testing security intelligence. It was also testing the limits of its power. Al-Yaqoubi’s arrest can be viewed as a dual test: Can the state use its security apparatus to reach senior faction cadres without the operation turning into an open confrontation? And will the factions accept the government’s shift from a policy of containment and coordination to a policy of imposing a fait accompli? This is why the subsequent handover of the man to Hashd Security provided a negotiated way out. The move allowed the government to demonstrate its ability to escalate while simultaneously preventing the initial escalation from turning into a larger confrontation.

But the factions’ response was clear. The intensity of the threats rose to levels that included talk of storming the Green Zone, while Hadi al-Amiri took a tense position in support of the factions. This means that the security test quickly turned into a broader political and security test of the limits of the government’s authority.

As for the statement by Abu Mujahid al-Assaf, the security official of Kataib Hezbollah, it was the most forceful. Its significance comes not only from its language but also from the context in which it was issued. During the recent period, Kataib Hezbollah had shown considerable flexibility toward government measures and had complied with several requests related to verifying or inspecting certain headquarters. However, the arrest of a senior figure from his home was viewed by the group as a shift from conditional cooperation toward an attempt to test the balance of power itself. This is why al-Assaf used the popular expression that “the nephew has seven strikes on his uncle’s chest,” before effectively announcing the end of these “strikes.” The political meaning here is more important than the expression itself: the space for flexibility that had allowed the factions to absorb state measures is beginning to narrow, and every new step could produce a corresponding counterstep.

The arrest of Abbas al-Yaqoubi can be viewed as the third confrontation between the al-Zaidi government and the factions, particularly Harakat al-Nujaba, within a short period.
