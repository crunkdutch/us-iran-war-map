🔗 | Three major developments:

1. The sanctions narrative returns, but Washington faces its own limits:

The latest discussion around secondary sanctions against Iran reveals something Washington rarely admits publicly: fully enforcing maximum sanctions carries serious risks for the U.S. economy itself.

Sanctions experts and international legal scholars have warned that aggressive secondary sanctions targeting countries and companies trading with Iran could have devastating consequences for the global financial system. Former U.S. Treasury sanctions expert Jason Pynes argued that secondary sanctions become an economic gamble when the U.S. economy is already under pressure, making America itself more vulnerable. According to him, the sectors most exposed would be technology, manufacturing, and finance.

The most revealing moment came when U.S. Treasury Secretary Kelly Bessent was asked why Monday’s announcement sounded more like a warning than immediate action. Her response was simple: she did not want to “blow up the global financial system.”

That statement exposes Washington’s dilemma. To inflict a crippling economic blow on Iran, the United States would likely have to sanction major Chinese banks and companies facilitating trade with Tehran, a move it has so far avoided because of the enormous consequences for the global economy.

The message is becoming increasingly clear: sanctions continue to be used as pressure, but fully implementing them carries costs that Washington itself appears reluctant to bear.

2. The same negotiation cycle returns, with the same faces and the same destination:

Once again, the Iranian negotiation machinery is back. The same talking points, the same media campaign, the same propagandists, and the same promise that this time America will respect its commitments.

Negotiation team's media propagandist Mohammad Marandi has already begun preparing the narrative, saying Iran only cares that the america fulfills its commitments under the Memorandum of Understanding or any future agreement. The obvious question is: Which agreement? For what purpose? And with what expectation of success?

The timeline speaks for itself.

Asim Munir arrives in Tehran. Oman immediately becomes the center of diplomacy. Iranian officials suddenly announce a temporary Hormuz agreement instead of maintaining the previous position. Then discussions begin about a permanent maritime route. None of this looks accidental. It looks coordinated.

Iran’s Deputy Foreign Minister Kazem Gharibabadi says the agreed transit corridor with Oman is seven miles wide, temporary, and will later be replaced by a permanent route after negotiations within 30 to 60 days. At the same time, Iran insists the Strait of Hormuz is not actually reopening.

Then comes the most revealing part.

On the very day this narrative emerged, the Gulf of Oman witnessed around 15 ship-to-ship transfer operations involving nearly 25 million barrels of crude oil, alongside refined petroleum products. Suddenly commercial traffic accelerates while officials insist nothing has reopened. So what exactly is happening?

A temporary two-way route for what? To move commercial shipping? To clear naval mines? To facilitate controlled maritime traffic? And if mines are being cleared, whose request initiated that process? Washington’s? Oman’s? Or someone else’s?

This is where the entire story becomes difficult to ignore.

If you design a strategic maritime passage with a partner while already knowing that partner lacks the capacity to withstand American pressure, then you have not designed a strategic partnership. You have designed an exit route for the day pressure arrives.

The enemy has been described as untrustworthy for decades. Precisely for that reason, negotiations were supposed to produce mechanisms that do not depend on America’s goodwill or the goodwill of America’s allies. Instead, the country is once again returning to promises, memorandums, guarantees, and diplomatic language.

The most dangerous part is not even the negotiations themselves.
