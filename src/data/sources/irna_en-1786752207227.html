⚡️ Iran calls for Caspian Sea states to condemn security threats

📌 Tehran, IRNA – Deputy Foreign Minister for Legal and International Affairs Kazem Gharibabadi calls on Caspian Sea littoral states to explicitly condemn any actions that threaten the security of the Caspian Sea, while stressing the need to protect it as a zone of peace, friendship and cooperation.


Speaking on Caspian Sea Day, Gharibabadi said August 12 marks the anniversary of the entry into force in 2006 of the Tehran Convention, a framework agreement for protecting the Caspian Sea’s marine environment that reflects the five coastal countries’ commitment to protecting the world’s largest lake.
​
Turning to security issues, Gharibabadi said attacks targeting the Caspian Sea threaten regional stability and the interests of all littoral states.
​
He cited a missile attack by the Israeli regime and the United States on facilities at Anzali Port in late March, as well as a Ukrainian drone attack on an Iranian commercial vessel in the Caspian Sea, as examples of threats to the region’s security.
​
“Such actions not only negatively affect regional peace and stability, but also highlight the need for solidarity among the Caspian littoral states in defending their common security,” he said.
​
Gharibabadi called on all Caspian coastal countries to explicitly condemn such actions and emphasize respect for national sovereignty and territorial integrity.
​
He described the Caspian Sea’s living aquatic resources as an important source of food security and livelihoods for coastal communities, noting that sustainable fishing, related industries and tourism all depend on the health of the sea.
​
Gharibabadi said the Caspian Sea is currently facing unprecedented challenges, particularly a worrying decline in its water level, which is affecting its ecosystem, ports and shipping, fisheries and other activities.
​
He said the decline in the water level is not merely a temporary or natural issue, but reflects the complex interaction between climate change and human intervention.
​
Gharibabadi stressed that scientific cooperation among the littoral states is essential to address these challenges, including continuous monitoring of water-level changes, environmental assessments, biodiversity studies and analysis of climate and hydrological trends.
​
He called for joint research programs, stronger regional scientific networks and greater use of universities and research centers to support sustainable management of the Caspian ecosystem.
​
He said Caspian Sea Day provides an opportunity for the littoral states to reconsider their shared responsibilities, adding that protecting the sea requires legal commitments, scientific cooperation, environmental management and regional coordination.
​
According to Gharibabadi, such cooperation is necessary to preserve the Caspian ecosystem, protect the livelihoods of coastal communities, and maintain long-term regional security.

In a post on his X account on Wednesday marking the 77th anniversary of the adoption of the four Geneva Conventions, Baqaei said that today, 12 August, marks the anniversary of the adoption of the four Geneva Conventions in 1949—the core international treaties that established the most essential rules of the law of armed conflict, namely 'humanity', 'distinction', 'military necessity', 'proportionality', and the prohibition of 'unnecessary suffering' in order to civilize humanity and humanize war.
​
He pointed out that the architects of these four treaties could scarcely have imagined that, in 2026—seventy seven years after the adoption of these instruments —the United States, host nation of the United Nations and a permanent member of the Security Council, would repeatedly threaten to attack the infrastructure, bridges, and power plants of another country, and would in fact commit such crimes.
​
Baqaei said that the international community, the States Parties to the Geneva Conventions, and Switzerland as the depositary of these instruments cannot remain indifferent to such unprecedented violations, which constitute clear war crimes.
​
He stressed that Common Article 1 of the Geneva Conventions does not merely obligate States to respect the Conventions; it requires them to “ensure respect” for them as well.
​
Baqaei said that silence in the face of explicit threats against civilian objects is not mere political passivity—it is the erosion of one of the most fundamental civilizational and legal achievements of the post-World War II era. Wherever the law falls silent before power, what collapses is not merely a bridge or a power plant, but humanity’s confidence in the possibility of the rule of law in international relations.
​
If the threat to destroy civilian infrastructure becomes normalized in international affairs, no state can any longer feel secure about the inviolability of its hospitals, electricity grids, bridges, water facilities, or other vital arteries against external aggression.
​
This is not merely a threat against one government; it is a regression of the entire legal order that humanity has built in its pursuit of civilization and the restraint of violence.
​
Yet for us Iranians, the answer remains clear: No storm can shift a mountain from its place, and no number of trials can break those who stand firm on the path of resolve.
